A driven session of the orchestrator2 explorer was reported as laggy, and then
as getting worse the longer it played. This is what the loop does under a
playhead, and it is worse than "laggy": at one cursor the playhead does not
miss *often*, it misses **everything**, for as long as the window takes to
fill.

A 480-row window declared from its start, a transport advancing one row per
frame period at 23.976 fps and wrapping — the cheap case, since a smooth
transport is what a fetcher can step for. Fraction of ticks whose row was
already resident, per five-second bucket, two repeats agreeing to three
decimal places:

| readers | 0 s | 5 s | 10 s | 15 s | 20 s | 25 s |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.95 | 1.00 |
| 2 | 0.92 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 |

Resident runway ahead of the playhead, same buckets: at one reader it is **0
rows for twenty seconds**; at two it is 215 inside the first bucket and never
falls below 65.

**The fill is not failing — it is being held to the playhead's own speed.**
Both arms end with 480 of 480 rows covered. At two readers the window is
effectively full within five seconds. At one it takes twenty, which is 24 rows
per second against the ~100 a sequential read of this footage sustains. That
factor of four is the GUI taking the cursor: every miss preempts the fill,
which pays a seek out and a seek back
(`2026.08.30-the-pressure-dispatcher-preempts-into-seeks`), and delivers
nothing while it does.

**And that is a closed loop, which is why it never recovers on its own.** The
playhead advances at the frame rate whether or not it is served. While the
fill is seeking it produces no rows, so the playhead gains on the frontier; a
frontier behind the playhead means the next tick misses too; a miss steals the
cursor again. The hit rate is not low, it is zero — every tick for twenty
seconds — because the loop admits no way out until the fill happens to
complete the window in spite of it.

**Two cursors do not mitigate this, they prevent it.** Reader 0 never takes an
interactive pick, so the fill reads sequentially throughout and gets 215 rows
in front before the transport has covered its first five seconds. After that
the playhead is reading out of the pool and the second reader has almost
nothing to do.

**The session that prompted this ran at one reader**, which was the explorer's
default, and its log carries the same signature: 11.2 s of a 19.8 s session in
seek latency, 21 fill seeks against 15 the GUI caused, 47 of 101 GUI decodes
landing after the playhead had moved on, and 25 frames resident at the end
against a 480-row declaration.

## What this does not establish

**That the felt complaint was progressive.** What is measured is a step — a
flat blackout, then recovery — not a ramp. The session that reported it landed
four windows, and a landing drops the pool and starts the blackout again, so a
person exploring restarts the clock every time they move and never reaches the
recovery. That is a plausible account of "worse the longer I played" and it is
an account, not a measurement; a driven session at two readers is what would
settle it.

**Anything about scrubbing.** The transport here advances by one, which is the
case the fetcher can step for. A hand that jumps is a different and already
measured workload
(`2026.08.30-a-second-cursor-that-overlaps-costs-a-scrub-nothing`).

**Anything about window size.** 480 rows at full frame. A 20-second blackout is
the time to fill 480 rows at the degraded rate; a smaller window or a coarser
form shortens it without touching the loop, and neither was run. The proxy
tier — a whole recording at one coarse form, which is what a scrub is supposed
to fall to — is absent from this folder entirely, and it is the mechanism that
would make a miss cheap rather than making misses rarer.

**One machine, one file, one form, no step running.**

## How to recreate

```
uv run --group tools --group experiments python experiments/orchestrator2-experiments/07-playback-divergence.py
```

The buckets are the result. A single hit-rate average over the whole run reads
as 0.3 at one reader and hides that it is composed of twenty seconds of zero
and ten of one.
