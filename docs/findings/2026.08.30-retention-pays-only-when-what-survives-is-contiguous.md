title: Retaining evicted frames pays double on a return, and only when what survives is one contiguous band
measured: 2026-08-30

`orchestrator2-experiments` README question 4 asked whether a victim cache
beats dropping every unpinned key, and pre-registered fragmentation as the way
a per-item ranking would fail. It fails that way. It fails harder than
pre-registered: a fragmented remnant is worth **nothing at all**, not merely
less.

Three landings — window A, window B, back to A — with a ceiling of 1.5 windows
so B's arrival forces eviction and what survives of A is the policy's
decision. 120 full frames a window, three interleaved repeats, medians:

**Exact return** (the walk's leg 5, landing back on A's first row):

| policy | decodes | wall s | rows resident | contiguous runs |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| drop-all (V1) | 120 | 1.516 | 0 | 0 |
| lru | 60 | **0.752** | 60 | 1 |
| contiguous | 60 | **0.750** | 60 | 1 |
| gdsize | 60 | 1.544 | 60 | 30 |
| belady (oracle) | 60 | 1.558 | 60 | 32 |

**Offset return** (landing back half a window along, which is what a person
returning to a place actually does):

| policy | decodes | wall s | rows resident | contiguous runs |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| drop-all (V1) | 120 | 1.625 | 0 | 0 |
| lru | 120 | 1.756 | 0 | 0 |
| contiguous | 120 | 1.751 | 0 | 0 |
| gdsize | 92 | 1.724 | 28 | 19 |
| belady (oracle) | 60 | **0.914** | 60 | 1 |

Walls are stable across repeats — the widest spread is 0.02 s on the
half-second arms and 0.2 s on the 1.7 s ones — so the 2× gaps are effects and
not this machine's drift.

**Retention halves the return leg, and contiguity is the whole of why.** LRU
and the contiguity rule both keep 60 rows in one band and both take 0.75 s
against drop-all's 1.52. GreedyDual-Size keeps *the same sixty rows* scattered
across 30 runs and takes 1.54 s — indistinguishable from having kept nothing.
Sixty scattered frames are worth zero frames. That is the dependent-cost
objection to per-item ranking, and it is not a tax on the policy, it is the
entire benefit.

**The cost is not seeks, which is where the pre-registration was half wrong.**
Seeks barely move (1 against 2). `fetch.STEP_WITHIN` is 60, so the fetcher
*steps* across a gap shorter than that, decoding and discarding every frame in
between; fragmentation therefore bills as wasted sequential decoding inside a
step, invisible to both the decode count and the seek count and visible only
in the wall. `runs_on_arrival` is the number that predicts the wall here, and
a future experiment about locality should report runs rather than seeks.

**GreedyDual-Size is refused for this workload.** It is the textbook policy for
items differing in cost and size, and this tree differs in both, which is why
it was proposed. It ranks items independently, and a frame's refetch cost
depends on whether its neighbour is resident, so it optimises the quantity it
can see and destroys the one that pays. It holds a full extra half-window of
memory to do it.

**Belady is the wrong oracle for an exact return, and this is informative
rather than a defect.** Under an exact return every evicted row is wanted again
at the same future moment, so MIN cannot discriminate and its choice among ties
scatters exactly as GD-Size's does — 32 runs, 1.558 s. Belady minimises *miss
count*; the cost here is *miss cost*, and the two come apart the moment
retrieval cost depends on what else was retained. An oracle that is beaten by
LRU is an oracle answering a different question.

**On the realistic return, every implementable policy is at or below break
even.** Offset by half a window, LRU and contiguity retain nothing useful —
the sweep fills anchor-first, so their most-recent band is the half the
returning consumer does *not* want — and both come out slower than dropping
everything (1.75 against 1.63), having paid 1.5 windows of memory for it.
GD-Size's 28 useful rows buy 0.1 s at best. Only the oracle wins, at 0.91 s,
and it wins by knowing *which* rows the next landing wants.

**So the lever is not the ranking.** The gap between every online policy and
the oracle on the offset return is the whole result, and it is a gap in
information rather than in algorithm: at eviction time the next landing has not
been declared, so nothing in the pool can know where the consumer will come
back to. That is a prediction problem — the application knows the playhead's
history and a replacement policy does not — and it is not solved by a better
victim rule.

## What this does not establish

**Anything about the walk's leg 5 as it stands.** The exact return is the
scripted scenario and flatters retention: a person coming back to a place does
not land on the frame they left. If leg 5 is used to justify retention, the
offset row of the second table is the honest one.

**A default.** `pool.py` still defaults to `DropAll`, which is V1's behaviour
and what every other number in this folder was taken against. Nothing here
beats it across both shapes, so changing the default would be choosing one
workload over the other on no evidence.

**Anything under contention.** No interactive consumer, no step: one sweep,
one reader, one form. Retention and scheduling would interact — a person
scrubbing changes which rows are pinned when the ceiling bites — and that is
the explorer's to measure. `victim_hits` reads zero throughout because nothing
in this topology calls `get`; the sweep holds and never serves.

**Anything about sizing.** One ceiling, at 1.5 windows. Whether a larger
ceiling makes the offset return pay for an implementable policy is unmeasured,
and the derived-eviction finding's caution applies: a consumer that may be
scrubbed declares its whole span, so ceilings and windows are not independent.

## How to recreate

```
uv run --group tools --group experiments python experiments/orchestrator2-experiments/04-victim-cache.py
```

`REPS` interleaves the arms. The two shapes are the result — running only the
exact return concludes that retention works, and running only the offset return
concludes that it does not.
