---
title: Preempting a sequential fill costs a seek out and a seek back, and rank is not what decides it
measured: 2026-08-30
where: experiments/orchestrator-experiments/explorer-logs/ — 034245Z (parked) and 034022Z (live) under declared rank, 035833Z (live) under derived rank; all carry `dispatch_trace`
status: measured in this tree (v4), via the orchestrator explorer
---

The orchestrator's dispatcher serves the highest-pressure declared position
not on hand, re-consulting after every decode, so preemption granularity is
one frame. Six window fills — three with the playhead parked, three with it
looping at 23.976 fps (`--live-playhead`), same code and same script — all
six under the declared ladder, which the last section then replaces:

| leg | wall | gui dec | tool dec | seeks | seek s | step s | s/seek |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| parked, A cold | 6.6 | 9 | 1 | 9 | 2.6 | 3.8 | 0.290 |
| parked, B seam | 6.6 | 7 | 1 | 7 | 2.3 | 4.2 | 0.332 |
| parked, A again | 6.3 | 5 | 1 | 6 | 2.1 | 4.1 | 0.344 |
| live, A cold | 35.1 | 129 | 47 | 92 | 28.3 | 6.7 | 0.308 |
| live, B seam | 22.4 | 77 | 26 | 57 | 17.4 | 4.9 | 0.305 |
| live, A again | 11.2 | 27 | 8 | 20 | 6.6 | 4.4 | 0.329 |

**The wall is a two-term cost model, not a spread.** Seconds-per-seek is
0.29–0.34 in every leg of both conditions, and the sequential term is
3.8–4.2 s for 480 frames wherever seeks are rare. So

    wall ≈ (480 sequential reads) + 0.31 s × seeks

and every leg above is predicted within a second by it. Nothing here is
noise: the six walls span 6.3 to 35.1 s because the six legs apply six
different interaction loads, and the wall is linear in the seeks that load
produces. An earlier reading of the same numbers as run-to-run variance was
wrong — a repeat of the live walk reproduced 35.1 / 22.4 / 11.2 against
34.2 / 23.6 / 10.8, leg for leg.

**Left alone, the sweep is a near-perfect sequential reader** — under the
declared ladder `fill:seek` is 1 per 480 frames in every leg of both
conditions. Every other seek is the dispatcher abandoning that run for
something higher-pressure and paying to get back; 86 of 96 role alternations
in the worst leg were immediately followed by a seek. Who is *billed* for the
return depends on who is served next, which is what made the tool look
culpable below — under the derived rank `fill:seek` becomes 45/26/10 for
exactly the seeks the tool used to own.

**Every preemption is a seek pair, and that is where the time goes.** A GUI
seek out is matched almost one-for-one by a seek back to wherever the sweep's
frontier was, because the fetcher has one cursor and the run it abandoned has
to be rejoined. Per leg, `gui:seek` against the return seek: 46/45, 31/26,
12/10. So an interruption costs about 0.62 s, not 0.31 s, and the sequential
term is not merely paused but re-entered.

**Rank is not what decides it, and the obvious fix bought nothing.** Under a
declared ladder the tool outranked the sweep feeding it and *all* of its
decodes were seeks — 81 of 81 here, 19 of 19 across four other sessions, at
195–366 ms. That reads as the tool buying by seek what the sweep was about to
hand it sequentially. Deriving the rank instead (a need inside a wider
declaration yields to it) removed those decodes completely: `by_pressure` goes
from `INTERACTIVE 246 / BACKGROUND 1125 / BATCH 79 / NEAR 2` to
`gui 242 / fill 1210`, and no tool-caused decode remains. **The walls did not
move** — 34.7 / 22.5 / 11.3 against 35.1 / 22.4 / 11.2, total seeks 91/57/22
against 92/57/20. The seeks simply changed owner, `tool:seek` becoming
`fill:seek` one for one. The tool's decodes had been the *return leg* of the
GUI's preemption, attributed to whichever node happened to be served next.

The derived rank is kept regardless, because a consumer asserting where it
ranks against declarations it cannot see is ADR-0007's falsified move in
another costume, and the anomaly it removed was real. But it is a
correctness fix, not a speed one, and anything expecting rank to recover the
wall is looking at the wrong term.

A GUI that moves smoothly is cheap and one that jumps is not, for the same
reason: of 129 GUI decodes in the worst leg only 44 were seeks, because the
transport advances by one and the fetcher steps. `stale` — a decode that
landed after the asker had moved on — was 153 of 246 INTERACTIVE decodes,
so most preemptions bought nothing at all.

What is left is the one rule that would actually move the wall: **do not
leave a sequential run for a position that run will reach anyway** — a
`STEP_WITHIN` check before committing to a seek — and, failing that, do not
pay to rejoin it, which means a cursor the interruption does not destroy
rather than a smarter queue. Neither is implemented.

Caveats. One topology, one file, one window size; `s/seek` here is the
whole-system figure and not decomposed against the 220–370 ms band measured
in `2026.08.21-uncut-seek-costs-a-gop-not-a-frame`. The `step s` term itself
rises with seeks (3.8 s to 6.7 s) because a seek lands on a keyframe and the
frames back to the target count as steps — the two terms are not fully
independent. Only runs postdating three fixes made the same day are used:
the transport spin, the duration-bar misattribution, and the `_land_at`
release-before-declare re-decode. Earlier logs in that directory are
contaminated by one or more; `explorer-logs/README.md` gives the
field-presence test for telling them apart.

## How to recreate

```
uv run --group experiments python experiments/orchestrator-experiments/explorer.py --walk
uv run --group experiments python experiments/orchestrator-experiments/explorer.py --walk --live-playhead
```

`dispatch_trace` in the log is the ordered `(t, role, position, how, ms)` of
every decode; the table above is that sliced by each leg's `window covered`
wall. `--window-seconds N` shrinks the window for a faster run: the seek and
alternation counts hold, the walls and memory numbers do not.
